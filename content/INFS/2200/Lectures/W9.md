# Week 9 — Query Optimisation

## Cardinality and selectivity

Cardinality $|R|$ is the number of tuples in relation $R$

For predicate $c$:

$$
sel(c)=\frac{|\sigma_c(R)|}{|R|}
$$

and the estimated result size is:

$$
|\sigma_c(R)|\approx |R|\times sel(c)
$$

A small selectivity value means few rows survive and is often called a highly
selective predicate

For uniformly distributed values with $V(A,R)$ distinct values:

$$
sel(A=v)\approx\frac{1}{V(A,R)}
$$

Real optimisers use catalogue statistics, null fractions, most-common values
and histograms because uniformity and independence assumptions often fail

## Equivalence rules

Useful transformations include:

$$
\sigma_{c_1\land c_2}(R)
=
\sigma_{c_1}(\sigma_{c_2}(R))
$$

$$
\sigma_c(R\bowtie S)
=
\sigma_c(R)\bowtie S
$$

when $c$ refers only to attributes of $R$

Joins are associative and commutative for inner-join semantics, giving the
optimiser multiple join orders, while outer joins require more care

## Heuristic optimisation

1. break conjunctive selections into separate predicates
2. push restrictive selections near their base relations
3. push projections down while retaining join and output attributes
4. replace a Cartesian product followed by a join condition with a join
5. perform the most restrictive joins early
6. use indexes and existing order where their total cost is lower

These rules reduce intermediate results, but cost-based comparison is still
needed because an early index probe or sort can be more expensive than a scan

## Estimation failures

Large estimated-versus-actual row differences often indicate:

- stale statistics
- correlated columns treated as independent
- skew hidden by coarse histograms
- predicates the estimator cannot model well

Bad cardinality estimates propagate upward and can change join order, join
algorithm and memory allocation

> [!TIP] Optimisation workflow
>
> Draw the initial tree, push down safe filters and projections, compare
> plausible physical plans, then validate estimates against actual execution
