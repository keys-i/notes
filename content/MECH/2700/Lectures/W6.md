# Week 6 - Iterative Linear Systems and Least Squares

## Conditioning

A system can be mathematically solvable yet extremely sensitive to small input
changes

For

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b
$$

the condition number is

$$
\kappa(A)=\lVert A\rVert\lVert A^{-1}\rVert
$$

| Condition number | Interpretation                                    |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Near $1$         | Well-conditioned                                  |
| Large            | Small data errors may cause large solution errors |
| Infinite         | Singular matrix                                   |

Perturbations in the right-hand side satisfy the bound

$$
\frac{\lVert\Delta\mathbf x\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf x\rVert}
\le
\kappa(A)
\frac{\lVert\Delta\mathbf b\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}
$$

This is a worst-case bound, not a prediction that every error is amplified by
exactly $\kappa(A)$

> [!EXAMPLE]- Nearly parallel equations
> The system
>
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> 1&1\\
> 0.99&1.01
> \end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}f_1\\f_2\end{bmatrix}
> =
> \begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> has $f_1=f_2=1$, but a small change in the support position produces a large
> change in the forces because the two equations are nearly parallel

## Vector and Matrix Norms

For $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n$

$$
\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_1=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|x_i|
$$

$$
\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_2
=
\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_i^2}
$$

$$
\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_\infty
=
\max_i|x_i|
$$

Common induced matrix norms are

$$
\lVert A\rVert_1
=
\max_j\sum_i|a_{ij}|
\qquad\text{maximum column sum}
$$

$$
\lVert A\rVert_\infty
=
\max_i\sum_j|a_{ij}|
\qquad\text{maximum row sum}
$$

$$
\lVert A\rVert_2
=
\sqrt{\rho(A^TA)}
$$

where $\rho$ is the spectral radius

```python
condition = np.linalg.cond(A)
```

## Why Iterate

Discretised engineering models often produce large sparse systems

Direct elimination can turn zero entries into nonzero entries, called
**fill-in**, increasing both memory and work

Iterative methods instead

- start from a guess $\mathbf x^{(0)}$
- apply a cheap update repeatedly
- stop when the change or residual is sufficiently small
- preserve locality and sparsity

The fixed-point form is

$$
\mathbf x=\mathbf g(\mathbf x)
$$

with iteration

$$
\mathbf x^{(k+1)}=\mathbf g(\mathbf x^{(k)})
$$

## Jacobi Method

From row $i$ of $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$

$$
a_{ii}x_i+\sum_{j\ne i}a_{ij}x_j=b_i
$$

rearrange for $x_i$

$$
x_i^{(k+1)}
=
\frac{1}{a_{ii}}
\left(
b_i-\sum_{j\ne i}a_{ij}x_j^{(k)}
\right)
$$

Every component in the new vector uses only values from the old vector

```python
def jacobi(A, b, x):
    x_new = np.empty_like(x, dtype=float)
    for i in range(len(b)):
        before = A[i, :i] @ x[:i]
        after = A[i, i + 1 :] @ x[i + 1 :]
        x_new[i] = (b[i] - before - after) / A[i, i]
    return x_new
```

> [!WARNING] Do not update Jacobi in place
>
> In-place updates immediately feed new values into later equations and turn
> the algorithm into Gauss-Seidel

## Gauss-Seidel Method

Gauss-Seidel uses each new value as soon as it becomes available

$$
x_i^{(k+1)}
=
\frac{1}{a_{ii}}
\left(
b_i
-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a_{ij}x_j^{(k+1)}
-\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}a_{ij}x_j^{(k)}
\right)
$$

```python
def gauss_seidel(A, b, x):
    x_new = x.copy()
    for i in range(len(b)):
        before = A[i, :i] @ x_new[:i]
        after = A[i, i + 1 :] @ x[i + 1 :]
        x_new[i] = (b[i] - before - after) / A[i, i]
    return x_new
```

Gauss-Seidel often converges faster than Jacobi because it uses fresher
information

> [!EXAMPLE]- Compare two sweeps
> Rearrange the lecture system as
>
> $$
> x_0=\frac{4-x_1+x_2}{2},
> \qquad
> x_1=\frac{10-x_0+x_2}{5},
> \qquad
> x_2=\frac{16-x_0-x_1}{6}
> $$
>
> starting from
>
> $$
> \mathbf x^{(0)}=[1,1,1]^T
> $$
>
> | Method       | $k$ | $x_0^{(k)}$ | $x_1^{(k)}$ | $x_2^{(k)}$ |
> | ------------ | --: | ----------: | ----------: | ----------: |
> | Jacobi       | $1$ |     $2.000$ |     $2.000$ |     $2.333$ |
> | Jacobi       | $2$ |     $2.167$ |     $2.067$ |     $2.000$ |
> | Gauss-Seidel | $1$ |     $2.000$ |     $1.800$ |     $2.033$ |
> | Gauss-Seidel | $2$ |     $2.117$ |     $1.983$ |     $1.983$ |
>
> Jacobi calculates a complete row from $\mathbf x^{(k)}$. Gauss-Seidel uses
> the new $x_0$ immediately when calculating $x_1$, then uses both new values
> when calculating $x_2$

## Convergence

A sufficient condition for convergence is strict row diagonal dominance

$$
|a_{ii}|>
\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|
\qquad
\text{for every row }i
$$

This condition is sufficient but not necessary

Reordering equations and unknowns can place larger coefficients on the
diagonal and turn a divergent rearrangement into a convergent one

> [!EXAM] Two-iteration questions
>
> - keep the stated order of unknowns
> - for Jacobi, finish the entire new vector before replacing the old one
> - for Gauss-Seidel, use a new component immediately
> - show the starting vector as iteration $0$
> - label each vector with its iteration number

## Successive Over-Relaxation

Successive over-relaxation scales the Gauss-Seidel correction by a relaxation
factor $\alpha$

$$
x_i^{(k+1)}
=
x_i^{(k)}
+
\frac{\alpha}{a_{ii}}
\left(
b_i
-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a_{ij}x_j^{(k+1)}
-\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}a_{ij}x_j^{(k)}
\right)
$$

|        Range | Name             | Typical effect                                 |
| -----------: | ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| $0<\alpha<1$ | Under-relaxation | Damps oscillation and may improve stability    |
|   $\alpha=1$ | Gauss-Seidel     | No relaxation                                  |
| $1<\alpha<2$ | Over-relaxation  | May accelerate an already convergent iteration |

An aggressive $\alpha$ can make convergence worse

## Stopping Criteria

Monitor both the iterate change and the residual

$$
\delta^{(k)}
=
\lVert\mathbf x^{(k+1)}-\mathbf x^{(k)}\rVert_\infty
$$

$$
\mathbf r^{(k+1)}
=
A\mathbf x^{(k+1)}-\mathbf b
$$

A robust loop also needs a maximum iteration count

```python
for _ in range(max_iterations):
    x_new = gauss_seidel(A, b, x)
    change = np.linalg.norm(x_new - x, np.inf)
    residual = np.linalg.norm(A @ x_new - b, np.inf)
    x = x_new
    if change <= tolerance and residual <= tolerance:
        break
else:
    raise RuntimeError("iteration did not converge")
```

## Over-Constrained Systems

When there are more independent equations than unknowns, an exact solution
usually does not exist

For

$$
A\boldsymbol\alpha\approx\mathbf b,
\qquad
A\in\mathbb R^{N\times m},
\qquad
N>m
$$

choose the coefficients that minimise the sum of squared residuals

$$
S(\boldsymbol\alpha)
=
\lVert A\boldsymbol\alpha-\mathbf b\rVert_2^2
$$

At the minimum

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_k}=0
$$

which produces the normal equations

$$
A^TA\boldsymbol\alpha=A^T\mathbf b
$$

> [!EXAMPLE]- Fit a line
> For $y=mx+c$, form the design matrix
>
> $$
> A=
> \begin{bmatrix}
> x_0&1\\
> x_1&1\\
> \vdots&\vdots\\
> x_{N-1}&1
> \end{bmatrix},
> \qquad
> \boldsymbol\alpha=
> \begin{bmatrix}m\\c\end{bmatrix}
> $$
>
> Then solve
>
> $$
> A^TA
> \begin{bmatrix}m\\c\end{bmatrix}
> =A^T\mathbf y
> $$

In practical code, prefer a dedicated least-squares solver because explicitly
forming $A^TA$ squares the condition number

```python
alpha, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)
```

## Method Selection

| Problem                        | Suitable method                               |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Small dense square system      | Pivoted direct solve                          |
| Large sparse square system     | Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel or a sparse solver       |
| Need simple parallel updates   | Jacobi                                        |
| Need faster serial convergence | Gauss-Seidel or tuned relaxation              |
| More equations than unknowns   | Least squares                                 |
| High condition number          | Revisit scaling, formulation and data quality |

---

> [!NOTE] Key ideas
>
> - Conditioning describes input sensitivity, not algorithm convergence
> - Iterative methods avoid the fill-in of direct elimination
> - Jacobi uses only the previous vector
> - Gauss-Seidel uses new components immediately
> - Strict diagonal dominance is a useful sufficient convergence condition
> - Relaxation trades convergence speed against damping
> - Least squares solves an over-constrained system by minimising residuals
